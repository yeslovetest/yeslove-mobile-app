Introduction 
Why do some people stay in relationships that clearly hurt them—emotionally, mentally, or physically? Most of us want to believe that love, trust, and respect are the foundations of any strong partnership. But sometimes, those elements are missing, and yet people remain committed. Why?


Psychologist Caryl Rusbult’s Investment Model offers some insight. It suggests that people weigh three things when deciding whether to stay or leave a relationship:


Satisfaction – Are they truly happy, or does the pain outweigh the joy?

Alternatives – Would life be better alone or with someone else?

Investments – What have they put in—emotionally, financially, socially?


While this theory applies broadly, there’s a powerful fourth force that’s often overlooked: culture.


Culture can be a source of strength, identity, and community—but it can also be the invisible cage that keeps individuals, especially women, stuck in unsafe or unfulfilling relationships. Let’s explore how culture shapes women’s experiences in love, particularly when it comes to domestic abuse and mental health—and how we can start shifting these dynamics for the better.

 



When Culture Keeps Women Trapped

Marriage as a Social Obligation

In many cultural contexts, marriage is not just a personal decision—it’s a responsibility to the family, the community, and tradition. In countries like China, Korea, and Japan, marriage is deeply tied to social order, status, and economic stability. Leaving a marriage, even in the face of abuse, is often seen as disgraceful—not only for the woman but for her entire family.


This pressure can silence victims. Speaking out or walking away may be perceived as dishonouring one’s parents or breaking family unity. For many women, the fear of social rejection or community shame outweighs the pain they endure in the relationship.


Economic Dependency and Power Imbalances

Financial independence is one of the biggest barriers to leaving abusive or emotionally unhealthy relationships. In places where traditional gender roles are still strongly enforced, women are often discouraged from working, owning property, or making financial decisions.


In parts of the Middle East, cultural and legal restrictions have historically kept women financially reliant on male family members. Even where laws have evolved, lingering norms continue to limit women’s economic agency.

In rural India, girls are often steered away from education and employment. As adults, this leaves them dependent on their husbands for survival—and vulnerable if those relationships become abusive.


Without income or access to their own money, women are often left choosing between enduring abuse or facing poverty, isolation, and even homelessness.



Harmful Practices That Reinforce Control

Certain cultural traditions, while normalised within communities, can create or worsen conditions for abuse:


Dowry systems in South Asia may lead to harassment, domestic violence, or even death if financial expectations are not met. In some cases, women are treated as financial liabilities rather than equal partners.

Child marriage, still practised in parts of Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, places girls in relationships with older, more powerful men. These girls are often denied education, autonomy, and the right to consent—making abuse and control almost inevitable.

Female genital mutilation (FGM), still common in some regions, is often justified as a rite of passage. In reality, it’s a violent practice that leaves long-lasting physical and psychological scars, reinforcing the idea that women’s bodies are to be controlled and modified for marriage.



Mental Health: The Invisible Weight

Beyond physical safety, there’s the often-unseen toll of emotional and psychological harm. In many communities, mental health is heavily stigmatised or misunderstood. Women experiencing anxiety, depression, or trauma due to abusive relationships are rarely encouraged to seek help.


In places without access to counselling or safe spaces, emotional wounds are left untreated. The result? A deep, silent suffering that makes it even harder to leave or rebuild.


And when therapy is unavailable or deemed inappropriate, women are often expected to simply endure—masking their pain for the sake of “honour,” “duty,” or “normalcy.”

 


The Power of Culture Done Right

While it’s important to name and challenge the cultural forces that harm women, it’s equally essential to recognise the parts of culture that uplift, protect, and nurture love.


Here are a few examples of cultural values and practices that support healthy relationships:


Ubuntu (Southern Africa): Ubuntu emphasises shared humanity and community care. It encourages couples to treat each other with empathy, patience, and mutual support.

Matrilineal Societies (e.g., Mosuo in China): These systems centre women in family structures, granting them decision-making power and social respect. Relationships are often based on equality rather than dominance.

Extended Family Support (Latin America): Intergenerational homes and tight-knit family networks often offer emotional and practical support, which can act as a buffer during relationship challenges.

Mindfulness and Compassion (Buddhist Cultures): These traditions promote emotional awareness, respectful communication, and intentionality—fostering emotional intelligence in intimate partnerships.


These cultural values show us what’s possible: traditions that empower rather than control, and community structures that uplift rather than isolate.

 


How We Can Create Change

Culture is not static. It evolves—and so can the ways we understand and navigate intimate relationships. Here’s how we can start building safer, more supportive environments for women:


1. Expand Access to Support

Establishing trauma-informed women’s shelters, counselling services, and peer support groups—especially in rural or conservative areas—can offer women a lifeline.


2. Empower Financial Freedom

Encouraging girls’ education, vocational training, and access to capital can help break cycles of dependency and abuse.


3. Enact and Enforce Legal Protections

Laws that criminalise domestic abuse, enforce child marriage bans, and support survivors must be strengthened and upheld—especially in communities where informal systems still dominate.


4. Collaborate with Cultural Leaders

Change is most effective when it comes from within. Working alongside elders, faith leaders, and respected voices can help reframe harmful practices in ways that still honour cultural identity.
